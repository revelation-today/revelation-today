In some languages "a book with seven seals" is a saying for a great mystery. The phrase comes from this chapter, though it does not mean quite what people assume.

## The book with the seven seals

<a name="92c0"></a>
In the throne room, the place of perfect worship, {{% bible val="a scroll with seven seals appears and draws all attention" link="rev:5,1" lang="en" %}}. But what is this seven-sealed scroll?

In the ancient world, sealed legal documents — such as a will sealed by seven witnesses — were opened by an appointed party to put their contents into effect, making them legally valid. But Revelation may also be drawing on the nearer image of the sealed scroll from the Old Testament, such as Ezekiel's scroll of lament (Ezek 2:9-3:3) or Daniel's book "sealed until the time of the end" (Dan 12:4) — a fitting background for a book about God's plan finally being unsealed.

So when the voice asks "who is worthy to open the scroll," it really means "who is able to put the contents of that scroll into effect?" It isn't entirely clear at this point what the scroll refers to concretely — you could call it the prophets, the Old Testament, or simply God's plan — but one thing follows: whoever opens it rules the world. The scroll works like a will. What stands in it is the promise God made to Adam, that human beings would reign over the earth; Adam forfeited it, so a human being has to be the one to open it, and whoever opens it inherits what is written there.[^scroll] {{% bible val="Since no one turns out to be able to open it, John is understandably in deep distress" link="rev:5,2-4" lang="en" %}}.

{{% bible val="But there is one who can open it: the Lion of Judah — the ancient title from Jacob's blessing over Judah's line, the ruler whose scepter would not depart until the obedience of all peoples belongs to him. That's what John hears — but what he sees is a slaughtered lamb. The two are aspects of the same reality: the apparently helpless lamb is the victorious, invincible lion" link="rev:5,5-7" lang="en" %}}. It is the {{% int_link val="mystery of a hero" link="/appl/topics/hero/a-real-hero" %}} — a challenging image, since every apocalyptic hope pictures a strong ruler who overthrows the forces of evil by sheer power.

Another surprising element is the worship of the Lamb. The previous chapter showed everything revolving around the throne; here, everything centers on Jesus — he is worshipped, and God allows it. That itself is telling: it shows Jesus is truly God, because only God may rightly be worshipped.

## The seven seals

<a name="4f8c"></a>
When Jesus opens the seals, he makes sure {{% int_link val="that the devil's plans are exposed" link="/expl/content/seals/the-mystery-of-the-four-horse-men" %}}. Satan wants to rule the world, and the first four seals show exactly what he intends to bring about on the earth.

The saints are not exempt from the hardship either — we will see why when the trumpets sound. Under the altar they {{% bible val="ask how long it will be" link="rev:6,9-11" lang="en" %}}, and the answer is not the one they asked for: they are given white robes and told to wait until the full number is complete. From here on, the tension between the saints' prayer and God's timing runs through the book. When the answer does come, it is not vindication but conversion: the testimony of the two witnesses turns most of their persecutors to God (11:13).

{{% bible val="What follows is cosmic upheaval, met by universal terror: everyone from kings to slaves hides and begs to be sheltered from the wrath of the Lamb — the same Lamb who was just pictured as slain. The one who conquered by dying is also the one whose coming wrath none can survive." link="rev:6,12-17" lang="en" %}}. This is truly the {{% int_link val="Day of the Lord" link="/expl/background/israel/the-day-of-the-lord" %}} — the Last Judgment, where everyone is held accountable and no one can hide. Which raises a fair question: if the end has just arrived, what are the trumpets and the bowls still doing after it? The answer is that the three series are not seven plus seven plus seven events in a row; they go over the same ground three times, each time harder. {{% int_link val="In sequence or retold?" link="/expl/background/structure/the-structure-of-the-book-of-revelation#chiasm" %}} takes that question up.

After the interruption of the {{% int_link val="144,000" link="/expl/content/army/the-144000" %}}, the seventh seal is opened, and it brings a half hour of silence. Silence carries its own meaning in the Bible, and in Jewish literature there's a strong connection between a time of silence in heaven and the moment God hears the prayers of the saints. This continues the idea from the {{% bible val="fifth seal, where the prayers of the saints come into focus" link="rev:6,9-11" lang="en" %}}. The prayers of the saints come up only a handful of times — the bowls of incense the elders hold ({{% bible val="5:8" link="rev:5,8" lang="en" %}}), the cry from under the altar ({{% bible val="6:9–11" link="rev:6,9-11" lang="en" %}}), and the incense carried up from the altar before the trumpets sound ({{% bible val="8:3–5" link="rev:8,2-5" lang="en" %}}) — but they connect the themes of the seals and the trumpets and plays a central role in both.

[^scroll]: Beale, p. 341: "God promised to Adam that he would reign over the earth. Although Adam forfeited this promise, Christ, the last Adam, was to inherit it. A human person had to open the book because the promise was made to humanity." No one else was worthy, because all stand under the judgment the book contains (5:3); Christ is worthy because he bore it (5:9), and those he represents share his reign (5:10).
