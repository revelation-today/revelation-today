Once you've wrestled with the details of Revelation 20 itself, a bigger question is usually waiting right behind it: which overall system does this chapter fit into? Historically, Christians have answered that question in three main ways, named for where they place Christ's second coming relative to the 1,000 years: premillennialism (before), postmillennialism (after), and amillennialism (the number describes something other than a future golden age).

It is tempting to set these out as three lists of arguments and count which list is longest. That is not how the disagreement actually works. All three views are held by careful readers who know the same texts, and each of them can answer the popular objections to it in its sleep. The disagreement lives in four specific places, and if you understand those four, you understand the debate — including why it has not been settled in eighteen centuries.

## The three views, briefly

<a name="9c2a"></a>
**Premillennialism.** Jesus returns first, defeats the beast and the false prophet, and then reigns bodily on earth for 1,000 years before Satan is released for one final rebellion and the new creation arrives. It comes in two main varieties — historic premillennialism, where the church goes through the tribulation, and dispensational premillennialism, which adds a {{% int_link val="pre-tribulation rapture" link="/expl/topics/others/the-rapture" %}} and a separate future for ethnic Israel.

**Postmillennialism.** The 1,000 years, taken as a long period rather than a literal count, describes an age in which the gospel progressively succeeds and the nations are, in large part, actually discipled — before Jesus returns to a world his gospel has already substantially won.

**Amillennialism.** The 1,000 years symbolically describes the whole church age, from Christ's first coming to his second — now — during which Satan is restrained from deceiving the nations as he once did, and believers already reign with Christ in a real but not yet visible sense. This is the reading the {{% int_link val="previous article" link="/expl/content/1000y/the-thousand-year-kingdom" %}} argues for.

None of the three is a modern invention. But the historical picture is not evenly balanced either, and it is worth being honest about that: the earliest identifiable readings of Revelation 20 that we can still name — Papias, Justin Martyr, Irenaeus, Tertullian — are millennial in something like the premillennial sense, and Justin is explicit that he holds it while acknowledging that other orthodox Christians do not (*Dialogue with Trypho* 80). Several of them wrote in or near Asia Minor, the book's own home region. The amillennial reading became the majority position later, above all through Augustine, who had held a millennial view himself before rejecting it.

## Crux 1: two comings to life, one verb

<a name="4f1c"></a>
This is the sharpest point in the whole debate, and it is where premillennialism is strongest.

{{% bible val="Revelation 20:4–5" link="rev:20,4-5" lang="en" %}} says that the martyrs *came to life* and reigned with Christ, and then, a few words later, that the rest of the dead *did not come to life* until the thousand years were ended. The Greek verb is the same in both clauses: **ἔζησαν**.

**The premillennial argument.** Everyone agrees that the second one means bodily resurrection from the grave. So the first must too — and if the first is a bodily resurrection, it happens before the thousand years, and there is a thousand-year gap between two bodily resurrections. Henry Alford's nineteenth-century formulation is still the one people reach for: if the same verb, in the same sentence, can mean spiritual rising in one clause and bodily rising in the next, then language has stopped conveying anything definite at all. Premillennialists then point at the book's own usage, which is narrower than it looks. This aorist form occurs only seven times in the whole New Testament, and four of them are in Revelation. Two of those four are not in dispute: {{% bible val="he became dead and came to life" link="rev:2,8" lang="en" %}}, of Christ, and the beast {{% bible val="whose sword-wound healed, and it came to life" link="rev:13,14" lang="en" %}}, of the counterfeit. Both mean a return from death. Aune's commentary — the standard critical treatment, and one of this page's own sources — reaches the same verdict and presses it further: the verb here carries the sense "raised from the dead, resurrected," and however awkward the notion of two resurrections may be, it is difficult to avoid concluding that the author has formulated exactly that.

**The amillennial answer** comes in four parts, and it is more substantial than it is usually given credit for.

- The subject of the verb in verse 4 is not bodies but **souls** — John sees "the souls of those who had been beheaded." Souls coming to life and reigning is a natural description of what happens to a martyr at death, not a description of a body leaving a grave.
- The word "first" is doing structural work, not counting. Revelation uses *first* and *second* to mark two **orders**, not two items in a series: the {{% bible val="first heaven and the first earth" link="rev:21,1" lang="en" %}} are the old order that passes away. So the "first resurrection" is the one belonging to the old order — the believer's passage at death into life with Christ — and the "second death" is the death belonging to the new order. The two pairs are deliberately crossed, which is why the passage can promise that over those in the first resurrection {{% bible val="the second death has no power" link="rev:20,6" lang="en" %}}.
- A verb can keep one meaning — *to come alive* — while what comes alive differs. Souls come alive at death; bodies come alive at the end.
- And Revelation does use this verb of spiritual life elsewhere. To Sardis: {{% bible val="you have a name that you are alive, and you are dead" link="rev:3,1" lang="en" %}} — the same pairing of living and dead that 20:4–5 makes, and unmistakably not about bodies.

**One more piece of evidence, and both sides claim it.** The one place in Revelation where God's servants plainly come back from death within the narrative is the two witnesses — and John describes it without this verb at all: a {{% bible val="breath of life from God entered them, and they stood on their feet" link="rev:11,11" lang="en" %}}. He is quoting {{% bible val="Ezekiel 37:10" link="ezk:37,10" lang="en" %}}, where the Greek runs "the breath entered them, **and they came to life**, and they stood on their feet." John keeps the entering and the standing almost word for word, and drops the verb in the middle. So this is not his fixed idiom for a body raised, and choosing it at 20:4 was not forced — but equally, you cannot argue that he would have written 20:4 the way he wrote 11:11 if he had meant the same thing there.

**Where that leaves it.** The amillennial answer is coherent, and the "first/second" observation is genuinely elegant. But it is fair to say that premillennialism has the more obvious reading of the sentence, and that amillennialism is paying a price here to keep the rest of its case. Readers who find the price too high usually end up premillennial, and they are not being careless when they do.

## What actually carries the theme

<a name="8b6d"></a>
Tracking one verb can mislead, because Revelation carries death-and-life mostly through a picture rather than a word: **slain, and then standing.**

The image is set at the centre of the book — {{% bible val="a Lamb standing as though it had been slain" link="rev:5,6" lang="en" %}} — and then handed on to everyone who follows him. The martyrs under the altar get the very participle used of the Lamb: {{% bible val="the souls of those who had been slain" link="rev:6,9" lang="en" %}}. The countless multitude out of the great tribulation is {{% bible val="standing before the throne" link="rev:7,9" lang="en" %}}. The two witnesses {{% bible val="stood on their feet" link="rev:11,11" lang="en" %}}. The 144,000 are {{% bible val="standing with the Lamb on Mount Zion" link="rev:14,1" lang="en" %}}, those who conquered the beast are {{% bible val="standing on the sea of glass" link="rev:15,2" lang="en" %}}, and at the end the dead are {{% bible val="standing before the throne" link="rev:20,12" lang="en" %}}.

Standing is the posture of vindication after being killed, and it belongs first to Jesus and then to his people.

The counterfeit runs alongside it — and notice which of the two uses the plain word. The beast has a head {{% bible val="as though slain to death, and its death-wound was healed" link="rev:13,3" lang="en" %}}, and {{% bible val="it came to life" link="rev:13,14" lang="en" %}}. The beast's resurrection is *stated*; the church's is *shown*.

This is worth holding onto through the argument that follows, because it means neither side settles Revelation 20 out of a concordance. Whatever 20:4 turns out to mean, the book's hope for its readers is not stored in that one verse. It is distributed across every scene in which somebody killed for the testimony ends up on their feet.

## Crux 2: what "bound" means

<a name="7d18"></a>
{{% bible val="Revelation 20:1–3" link="rev:20,1-3" lang="en" %}} piles up four verbs on Satan: an angel **seized** him, **bound** him, **threw** him into the abyss, and **shut and sealed** it over him.

**The premillennial argument.** No one seals a door over someone who is still walking around outside. Four verbs of total incapacitation, plus an abyss, plus a seal, do not naturally describe a restriction on one department of a devil's activity. And the New Testament goes on describing him as active: he {{% bible val="prowls like a roaring lion, looking for someone to devour" link="1pe:5,8" lang="en" %}}, and he is called {{% bible val="the god of this age, who has blinded the minds of unbelievers" link="2co:4,4" lang="en" %}}.

**The amillennial answer.** The text supplies its own limit, in a purpose clause most summaries leave out: he is bound **"so that he should not deceive the nations any longer"** — one named effect, not a general disappearance. And that effect is exactly what changed. Before Christ, the knowledge of God was largely confined to one nation; after Pentecost the gospel goes to the nations and they come in. Jesus described his own ministry in precisely these terms: no one can plunder a strong man's house unless he first {{% bible val="binds the strong man" link="mat:12,29" lang="en" %}}, and {{% bible val="I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" link="luk:10,18" lang="en" %}}. On this reading the world mission is not evidence against the binding; it is the binding, visible.

The rejoinder to Peter's roaring lion follows from the same distinction: Peter is describing danger to *individuals*, and 20:3 is about deceiving *nations as such*. Both can be true at once.

**Where that leaves it.** This crux runs the other way from the first one. The purpose clause is in the text, and it does limit the scope; premillennialism has to treat a stated limitation as if it were not there. But the sheer weight of the imagery is a real argument, and "bound, but only in one respect" will always sound like less than the picture shows.

## Crux 3: does chapter 20 follow chapter 19?

<a name="47e1"></a>
**The premillennial argument** is usually reported as resting on the little word "and" that opens chapter 20. That is the weakest form of it. There are two stronger ones.

First, **20:10 appears to look back at 19:20**. The devil is thrown into the lake of fire "where the beast and the false prophet *are* also" — a phrase that seems to presuppose that the events of 19:20 have already happened. (The Greek has no verb there; translators supply one. Amillennialists press exactly that point: an elided verb carries little temporal weight.)

Second, and more substantially, **Revelation 20 tracks the order of Ezekiel**. Ezekiel moves from the resurrection of the dry bones (37), to the assault of Gog and Magog (38–39), to the temple and the restored city (40–48). Revelation 20 moves from coming to life and reigning (20:4), to Gog and Magog (20:8), to the city (21–22) — naming Gog and Magog outright, which is not a common Old Testament phrase. If John is following a source's sequence, that is evidence of sequence.

**The amillennial answer.** Recapitulation is not a device invented for this chapter; it is the book's normal habit, argued at length in the {{% int_link val="previous article" link="/expl/content/1000y/the-thousand-year-kingdom" %}}. Chapter 12 has already told the story of Satan's fall and the church's protected period, and chapter 20 tells it again from a different angle. The decisive point is that the battle of chapter 19 and the battle of chapter 20 both draw on {{% bible val="the single final battle of Ezekiel 38" link="ezk:38" lang="en" %}}–{{% bible val="39" link="ezk:39" lang="en" %}}: if they are two different battles, John has used one Old Testament battle twice for two separate events. As for Ezekiel's order, the correspondence is thematic — John draws on Ezekiel throughout the book without following him chapter by chapter. And there is a concrete instance: Ezekiel's valley of dry bones is the resurrection chapter that opens the sequence, and John has already spent it at {{% bible val="11:11" link="rev:11,11" lang="en" %}}, nine chapters before the millennium, quoting it almost word for word. If he were following Ezekiel's order, he used its first movement early.

**Where that leaves it.** The Ezekiel argument cuts both ways and is a good illustration of why this debate persists: the same set of allusions supports "two battles in sequence" and "one battle told twice" about equally well, and which one you find natural depends on how much recapitulation you already think the book does.

## Crux 4: how much of the promise lands inside history?

<a name="b3f5"></a>
This is where postmillennialism lives, and its case is much wider than the Great Commission.

**The postmillennial argument.** A long line of Old Testament promises describes an outcome inside history, not after it: the nations given to the Son as {{% bible val="his inheritance" link="psa:2,8" lang="en" %}}; {{% bible val="all the ends of the earth remembering and turning to the LORD" link="psa:22,27" lang="en" %}}; {{% bible val="all kings falling down before him, all nations serving him" link="psa:72,8-11" lang="en" %}}; the nations streaming to the mountain of the LORD and {{% bible val="beating swords into ploughshares" link="isa:2,2-4" lang="en" %}}; {{% bible val="the earth full of the knowledge of the LORD as the waters cover the sea" link="isa:11,9" lang="en" %}}; the stone that becomes {{% bible val="a great mountain and fills the whole earth" link="dan:2,35" lang="en" %}}.

The New Testament picks the same thread up. The Great Commission expects {{% bible val="all nations" link="mat:28,18-20" lang="en" %}} to be discipled, not merely sampled. The leaven parable is not about growth in general but about completion — the woman hides it {{% bible val="until the whole was leavened" link="mat:13,33" lang="en" %}}. And Paul's argument in Romans 11 turns on a future that is larger than the present: if Israel's stumbling meant riches for the world, how much more {{% bible val="their fullness" link="rom:11,12" lang="en" %}}, when {{% bible val="all Israel will be saved" link="rom:11,25-26" lang="en" %}}.

**The two standard objections, and what postmillennialists say back.**

*"Revelation shows evil escalating, not receding — the beast makes war on the saints and conquers them."* This objection assumes a particular reading of Revelation, and most contemporary postmillennialists do not hold it. They are **partial preterists**: they read the beast, the tribulation, and the judgments as describing the first century and the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, already past. On that reading Revelation is not a forecast of our future at all, and the objection does not reach them. Whether partial preterism is right is a separate argument — see {{% int_link val="preterist, historicist, futurist or idealist" link="/expl/background/literature/preterist-historicist-futurist-or-idealist" %}} — but it has to be made, not assumed.

*"History hasn't gone that way."* Postmillennialists reply that this measures a claim about the whole age against one or two centuries, and usually against Europe. The same period contains the largest numerical expansion of Christianity in its history, in Africa, Asia and Latin America. They also grant setbacks and long reversals; the claim is about trajectory across the age, not a smooth upward line.

**The strongest reply to postmillennialism** is not "history disagrees" but Jesus's own parable of {{% bible val="the wheat and the weeds" link="mat:13,24-30" lang="en" %}}, where both grow together until the harvest and separation is explicitly deferred to the end. Postmillennialists note that {{% bible val="the field is the world" link="mat:13,38" lang="en" %}} and that the parable fixes no proportions — it forbids premature separation rather than promising a permanent stalemate. That is a fair reply, though it concedes that the parable is at least not evidence *for* the view.

**And where premillennialism sits on this crux.** Dispensational premillennialism answers the same question differently: the land and nation promises to ethnic Israel are concrete and unrepealed, so they need a concrete earthly kingdom to land in, and the millennium is it. Amillennialism answers that they land in Christ and in the church as Israel's heir — {{% bible val="all the promises of God find their Yes in him" link="2co:1,20" lang="en" %}}, and those who belong to Christ are {{% bible val="Abraham's offspring, heirs according to promise" link="gal:3,29" lang="en" %}} — and then finally in the new creation, which is where Isaiah's long life and ordinary work and fruitful ground actually belong. See {{% int_link val="Israel and the church" link="/expl/background/israel/the-church-is-part-of-israel" %}}.

That last exchange also settles the {{% bible val="Isaiah 65" link="isa:65,18-25" lang="en" %}} question that premillennialism raises: the passage describes long life and ordinary work but people still dying, which sounds like neither this age nor the final state — except that Isaiah introduces it himself as {{% bible val="new heavens and a new earth" link="isa:65,17" lang="en" %}}, the same phrase Revelation later uses for the final state.

## Where this site lands, and what it costs

<a name="c5e2"></a>
The {{% int_link val="previous article" link="/expl/content/1000y/the-thousand-year-kingdom" %}} argues for amillennialism, mainly on cruxes 2 and 3: the binding has a stated scope, and the same Ezekiel battle standing behind both chapter 19 and chapter 20 is hard to explain if they are two events. Add to that how closely chapter 20 tracks chapter 12's account of Satan's fall, and the recapitulation reading is the one that asks least of the rest of the book.

The honest cost is crux 1. One verb, twice, a few words apart, meaning two different kinds of coming-to-life is the hardest thing amillennialism asks of a reader, and the answer above — souls rather than bodies, *first* and *second* marking two orders — is an argument, not a knock-down. It should be said plainly that Aune's commentary, cited among this page's sources, decides that crux against the reading argued here. A reader who weighs that sentence more heavily than the book's recapitulation habit will land premillennial, and will be reasoning properly.

{{% int_link val="Bock, Three Views on the Millennium and Beyond" link="/about/ressources/index.html#bock_millennium" %}} is the place to see each case made at full strength by someone who actually holds it, with the others responding — which is the only format in which this argument is worth having.

A few questions worth sitting with:

- In {{% bible val="Revelation 20:4–5" link="rev:20,4-5" lang="en" %}}, does the same verb have to carry the same sense both times — and if it does, what follows?
- Which of the three pictures of "1,000 years" makes the best sense of Revelation's other numbers — the {{% bible val="144,000" link="rev:7,4" lang="en" %}}, the {{% bible val="42 months" link="rev:13,5" lang="en" %}}, the {{% bible val="1,260 days" link="rev:12,6" lang="en" %}}? Are those head-counts and calendars, or symbols?
- If Satan is bound, what exactly is the binding stopping — and can you point at the difference it has made?
- How much of what the Old Testament promises to the nations do you expect to see happen inside history, and how much only afterwards? Your answer to that question probably decides your answer to all the others.
