Armageddon: a catastrophe that will destroy the world? No — because, whatever real geography stands behind the name, John is using it as a symbol, not pinpointing a literal battlefield. Want to know why? Read on.

## The translation

<a name="02ee"></a>
The term Armageddon is mentioned only once {{% bible val="in the Bible," link="rev:16,16" lang="en" %}} with the note that it's a Hebrew term — though the interlinear text itself offers no further insight into what it means. So let's work through it step by step.

"Har" means "mountain" in Hebrew, but which mountain? Megiddo itself sits on a tell, not a prominent mountain, which is part of why more than one derivation has been proposed. Three meanings have been proposed, and each of them is worth hearing.

It can mean "mount of assembly," drawn from {{% bible val="a passage in Isaiah" link="isa:14,13" lang="en" %}} describing the fall of Babel.[^assembly] The verse just before that reference — Babel pictured as "fallen from heaven, O Day star, son of Dawn" — actually describes the powers behind Babel more than Babel itself, and may well refer to Satan, though that reading became standard only through later Christian tradition; the immediate context names the king of Babel as the target of the taunt, and many modern commentators read the "Day Star" language as stock ancient Near Eastern imagery for an overreaching king, applied to Satan only as a later typological extension. The whole chapter is a {{% bible val="mockery" link="isa:14,3-4" lang="en" %}} of the invincible, utterly destroyed.

It can also mean "mountain of Megiddo", the most common reading.[^megiddo] This one takes longer to unpack, since a great many events happened at Megiddo and its mountain.

And finally, it can mean "mountain of cutting down", or of slaughter. This is the oldest explanation we have: the first Greek commentators on Revelation read it this way, from Zechariah 12:11, where the Greek Old Testament speaks of the plain "of that which is cut down".[^slaughter]

Whichever meaning is heard, the name is mockery. The kings of the whole world bring their huge army to Megiddo, of all places — the place where, again and again, the weak won and the mighty fell. The bowl has already begun the joke: the Euphrates dries up to open the way for "the kings from the east" (16:12). That was Rome's own nightmare — an invasion from beyond the Euphrates, in popular rumour led by a returning Nero — and in the end it is the beast and his allied kings who turn on Babel and destroy her (17:16).[^east] The nations think they are gathering to wipe out God's people, but it is God who gathers them, to meet their judgment.

The three meanings may add one more layer. John tells us the name is Hebrew (16:16), which invites the reader to listen to what it means. Heard as a wordplay, like the taunt over Babel in Isaiah 14, the mountain of assembly where the kings gather turns out to be the mountain of their own cutting down. No commentator we know of treats the name as an intended pun, so this remains a suggestion; the mockery does not depend on it.[^pun]

## Deborah and Jael

<a name="4d88"></a>
The first mention of a battle near Megiddo concerns the judges {{% bible val="Deborah and Barak" link="jdg:4" lang="en" %}}. You may know the story, but do you remember Jael and her role in it? Let's start from the beginning.

Israel was, as so often in the book of Judges, {{% bible val="under a foreign ruler who was oppressing them" link="jdg:4,1-3" lang="en" %}}. Deborah {{% bible val="calls a man named Barak to follow God's promise and free Israel, since God had promised to deliver them" link="jdg:4,3-7" lang="en" %}}, but Barak is unsure and will only go if Deborah comes with him. She agrees — but not without announcing {{% bible val="that he will not land the final strike against their army commander Sisera; a woman will do that instead" link="jdg:4,8-9" lang="en" %}}. If you're thinking that woman is Deborah, you'd be wrong. This is where Jael comes in.

After Barak defeats the army, Sisera flees and seeks refuge in Heber's household, since Heber's family were on good terms with Sisera's king. It doesn't save him: {{% bible val="Heber's wife Jael takes the opportunity to kill Sisera as he sleeps" link="jdg:4,15-21" lang="en" %}}.

## Elijah

<a name="d43b"></a>
The mountain near Megiddo is called Carmel, and {{% bible val="this is one of the most famous stories in the Old Testament: one prophet of God standing against several hundred prophets of Baal" link="1ki:18" lang="en" %}}.

Some background first. King Ahab of Israel, together with his wife Jezebel of Sidon (a kingdom north of Israel), introduced the cult of Baal, the god of fertility, one of whose main duties was to bring rain — without which there would be no harvest. Queen Jezebel {{% bible val="hunts down the prophets of God until only one seems to be left: Elijah" link="1ki:19,10" lang="en" %}}. After Elijah's prophecy, the rain stops for three and a half years, and the whole conflict comes to a head on Mount Carmel, where he faces several hundred prophets of Baal and defeats them.

The story doesn't end there, though. {{% bible val="The queen is furious and wants him dead; Elijah, in turn, gives up and wants to die, but God calls him back out to anoint Elisha and Jehu." link="1ki:19" lang="en" %}} Along the way, God gently corrects Elijah's despair: he had thought he was the last faithful one left, but God reveals seven thousand in Israel who had never bowed to Baal — the same "weak conquer the invincible" pattern this whole cluster of stories keeps tracing.

When Jehu, {{% bible val="the commander of the army, is finally anointed" link="2ki:9" lang="en" %}}, he goes on to wipe out Jezebel's entire network as far as Judah. In the process, Jehu crosses paths with the king of Judah and shoots him — and the king of Judah dies in, of all places, Megiddo.

## Josiah

<a name="a257"></a>
{{% int_link val="There is a separate entry for this" link="/expl/content/bowls/armageddon-and-the-battle-of-karkemish" %}}, but the short version is this: a king gives his life fighting the king of Egypt, who was on his way to help the Assyrians against the rising Babylonian Empire. A few notes are worth adding.

Assyria had been oppressing Israel constantly up to this point, and stopped only because Babel started causing it trouble instead. Egypt, meanwhile, occupied an odd place in Israel's story — it was the place Israel had come out of slavery, yet it kept serving as the {{% bible val="backup plan for Israel, one that usually ended in disappointment" link="2ki:18,21" lang="en" %}}. It seems Josiah was the first king of Israel ever to march out against Egypt.

## Conclusion

<a name="c932"></a>
Armageddon represents a paradox that runs throughout the New Testament, and especially through the book of Revelation: the invincible is destroyed, and the weak conquer. The stories above trace that same pattern again and again.

The place of death mocks the one who conquered all. A housewife defeats an army commander. A single prophet turns all Israel back to God and defeats hundreds of the evil queen's prophets. A single commander overthrows his own king and the neighboring king without any real preparation. A king of a tiny kingdom fights a world power, is defeated and killed — and yet his people's story does not end there. It's the same pattern Paul names directly — "God chose what is weak in the world to shame the strong" (1 Corinthians 1:27) — and the same reversal pictured at the throne itself, where the Lion of Judah turns out to be a slaughtered Lamb (Revelation 5:5–6).
