When Israel first came to Egypt, they were welcomed. The next time we read about them, they are slaves. What happened in between?

## It all began with the Hyksos

<a name="31f6"></a>
The story starts with the Hyksos (literally “Rulers of Foreign Lands”)[^hyksos], a group that grew in influence as more and more Asian tribes — mainly from Canaan — mixed with the Egyptian population. Their presence kept growing until, by 1720 BC, they held full control over the eastern Nile Delta and even had their own capital, Avaris.

From there they expanded southward, and in 1674 BC they conquered Memphis, the former Egyptian capital. The [Hyksos thus formed the 15th and 16th dynasties of Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Intermediate_Period_of_Egypt), and under their rule, Semites such as Joseph were able to rise to high positions.

## Never again

<a name="dc6a"></a>
The Hyksos were finally defeated around 1550 BC, and Egypt was determined never to let a second Hyksos takeover happen. That determination is what drove Pharaoh to keep the Israelites small and weak.[^neveragain]

Several details suggest that Joseph himself lived during the Hyksos period:

- The {{% bible val="land of Goshen, in the northeast of Egypt, was close to the palace at the time Joseph was there" link="gen:45,10" lang="en" %}}.[^goshen]
- Israel was later forced to {{% bible val="build the cities of Pithom and Ramses" link="exo:1,11" lang="en" %}}, most likely under [Ramses II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramesses_II), who rebuilt the northeast — the very region {{% bible val="the Israelites eventually moved out from" link="exo:12,37" lang="en" %}}.[^ramses]
- Ramses’ father, Sethos I, built a summer palace near Avaris, the former Hyksos capital. That detail also explains how Moses could be found so quickly by the princess: the palace was still close to Goshen.[^seti]

[^hyksos]: Sarna, *Exploring Exodus*, pp. 15–16. The name means “Rulers of Foreign Lands” and strictly applied only to the chiefs themselves; the wider use came later. Sarna stresses that they were “not organized invaders” but “a conglomeration of ethnic groups, among whom Semites predominated, who infiltrated into the land over a long period of time in ever-increasing numbers, probably coming from Canaan”. The dates are his: in control of the eastern Delta with their capital at Avaris by ca. 1720, Memphis taken by a Hyksos king with the Semitic name Salitis by ca. 1674, and the XVth and XVIth Dynasties theirs. So is the point about Joseph: “the rise of Joseph to power and the migration of the Hebrews fits in well with what is known of the era of Hyksos rule”.

[^neveragain]: The expulsion was completed by Amose, ca. 1552–1527, who took Avaris and drove the Hyksos back into Canaan (Sarna, p. 16). The lasting effect is his as well, p. 17: “the danger of foreign invasion, especially from Asia via the eastern Delta, haunted her thereafter and could never again be smugly ignored or underestimated”. The Semitic population, he adds, was not driven out with its rulers — it went on living in the Delta through the XVIIIth and XIXth Dynasties.

[^goshen]: Sarna, p. 9, reads Genesis 45:10 and 46:28–29 the same way: the royal administration was located nearby, and “the palace was within easy access of Goshen”.

[^ramses]: Sarna, p. 10. The eastern Delta had been neglected through the two hundred odd years between the collapse of Hyksos rule and the XIXth Dynasty (ca. 1306–1200), and it was Rameses II (ca. 1290–1224) “who set up his capital here in his newly built city that he named for himself”. He adds Genesis 47:11, which puts Israel in “the region of Rameses” — anachronistic in context, but “a reflex of a genuine historic reality” — and Psalm 78:12, 43, which puts them in the “plain of Zoan”, that is Tanis in the north-eastern Delta.

[^seti]: Sarna, p. 18: “the first effective king of the XIXth Dynasty, Sethos (Seti) I (ca. 1305–1290 B.C.E.), built himself a summer palace just north of Avaris that was intended to form the nucleus of a new suburb”. Not long after his coronation Rameses II visited his father’s “freshly erected palace near Avaris” and announced the new capital there (p. 19). That the palace stayed close by is Sarna’s argument too, p. 10: Miriam could run home and fetch her mother, and the pharaoh could summon Moses and Aaron in the middle of the night, “which indicates that their domiciles were not too far away”.
