The new Jerusalem comes down, and it is three things at once: a city, a garden, and a bride.

Its length, width and height are equal - a perfect cube. There was one other cube in the whole
Bible: the Holy of Holies, the single room where God was nearest and almost nobody was let in.
The old temple had a flaw - you could be near or far depending which room you stood in. So the
new city is *entirely* Holy of Holies. There is no far part any more.

There is a river, and a tree whose leaves are for the healing of the nations. That is Eden,
lost on the third page of the Bible and handed back on the last.

But watch what God does not do. He does not put everyone back in the garden and write off the
rest. After Eden, people built cities - out of fear, because they were on their own. And God
does not demolish the city. He moves into the middle of it.

He takes what human beings built out of fear, and lives there.

That is the answer to the thing you have been carrying. Not that the world was fine. Not that
it gets scrapped and replaced with something unrelated. That what people made under the worst
conditions is kept, healed, and inhabited.

The sentence the whole book has been walking toward: *Look - God's dwelling place is now among
the people, and he will dwell with them.*

Every tear wiped away. Not forgotten - wiped. Which means someone is close enough to do it,
and knows what each one was for.

And it ends not with a warning but an invitation. The Spirit and the bride say, *Come.*
Whoever is thirsty, come. Whoever wishes, take the water of life, free.

Twenty-two chapters of dragons and beasts and bowls, and the last word is *come*.

You are not asked to feel hopeful. You are asked whether you want a drink.
