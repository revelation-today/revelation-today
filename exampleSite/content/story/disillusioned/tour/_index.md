Here is Revelation from beginning to end, told for someone who has run out of patience with
promises. Nothing is skipped and nothing is sweetened. Where the book is bleak, it stays
bleak. The claim it makes at the end is a real claim, and you are free to put it down.

One thing worth saying before we start: the people this was written for were not naive. They
had already lost.

# How everything started

In the beginning everything was whole. God made a world that worked and put people in it, not
as staff but as company, and walked with them in it.

It broke almost immediately, and it broke in a way worth naming precisely, because it is the
same break you are looking at now: people decided they would rather have what God gave than
God, and took charge of their own security. Driven out of the garden, their children built
cities and kingdoms - not out of ambition so much as fear. Something had to be built, because
nobody was coming.

God did not give up on them, which is the part that is hard to believe and easy to test. He
went to one man, Abraham, and asked for trust. Out of Abraham came a nation, Israel, and
through Israel a long and mostly disappointing history of people failing and God not leaving.

That history matters here for one reason. If you want to accuse God of being absent, you are
in a long tradition, and much of it is inside the Bible itself. The Psalms ask where he is.
The prophets ask how long. Job never gets his explanation. Revelation is the last book in a
library that has been asking your question for a thousand years without pretending it was
answered.

# The promise, and what it cost

Israel was given a job: show the world what God is like. It went badly. They were conquered,
deported, occupied, and eventually annexed by Rome, and the promises stayed unfulfilled long
enough that reasonable people stopped waiting.

Then God came himself, in a way nobody had planned for - as a baby, in an occupied province,
to a family who could not afford a room. He grew up, taught for three years, healed people,
made powerful men angry, and was executed by the state as a criminal.

Understand what that looked like on the day. It looked like the end of it. It looked like
what always happens when someone decent goes up against power: power wins, and the story
closes.

Three days later the tomb was empty.

That is the hinge of everything that follows, and Revelation never argues for it. It assumes
it, the way you assume the ground. If it did not happen, the rest of this is a beautiful
book about nothing.

# An old man on a rock

Sixty years on, an old man named John is on Patmos, a bare island in the Aegean. He is there
because he would not say Caesar was Lord.

Look at the scoreboard at this moment. Jesus rose, and Rome still runs the world. The church
is small, poor, and mostly frightened. John is on a rock, and his friends are dead. If you
had been asked in that year to say who was winning, you would not have said the Christians,
and you would not have been being cynical. You would have been reading the evidence.

Then the sky opens.

He turns toward a voice like a trumpet and sees someone standing among seven lampstands -
eyes like fire, feet like bronze from a furnace, a face too bright to look at. John does not
worship. He collapses like a dead man.

A hand comes down on his shoulder. *Do not be afraid. I am the First and the Last. I was
dead, and look - I am alive for ever, and I hold the keys of death.*

Notice what is not said. He is not told he was wrong about Rome. He is not told the suffering
was a misunderstanding. He is told who is holding the keys.

# Seven letters to seven ordinary churches

The first thing this enormous figure does is dictate mail - to seven small congregations in
seven unremarkable towns, each addressed by name, each seen exactly as it is.

Two of them are told nothing is wrong: the poorest one and the weakest one. The one told it
is in real danger is the comfortable one that thinks it needs nothing.

If you left a church, read those seven again. Jesus's own assessment of churches is harder
than yours is, and he does it without leaving.

# The room behind the room

Then a door opens in heaven, and John is taken through it.

There is a throne, and someone on it, and around it a rainbow - the sign given to Noah, a
promise about not ending the world. Around that, creatures and elders, and the sound is not
argument or command. It is singing.

Here is the claim this book is making, and it is worth stating flatly, because everything else
depends on it: the world you are looking at is not the whole room. There is another room, it
is not empty, and the person in it has not left. You cannot see it from here. That is the
problem, and Revelation does not pretend otherwise.

Then comes a scroll, sealed seven times, holding God's plan to set the world right - and
nobody in all creation is found worthy to open it.

John weeps. Openly, in the throne room. If the scroll stays shut, nothing changes ever.

An elder tells him to stop crying: the Lion of Judah has conquered. John turns to see the
Lion.

What he sees is a Lamb, standing, and looking as if it had been slaughtered.

This is the centre of the book. The one who wins is the one who was killed. Not the strongest
- the one who let it be done to him. If you have concluded that force is the only thing that
ever really settles anything, this image is the argument against you, and it is the only
argument the book makes.

# Four riders, and a question with no date

The Lamb opens the seals, and out come four riders: conquest, war taking peace from the earth,
famine with food priced out of reach, and death.

Do not rush past who is opening them. The Lamb is. That is not the same as saying he sends
them. It means that not one of these things is happening outside his reach - which is a
colder comfort than most tellings admit, and it is the actual comfort on offer.

Then the fifth seal, and this is the one to sit with. There is no rider. There are people
under an altar, killed for their testimony, and they ask one question:

**How long?**

They are given white robes and told to wait a little longer.

That is the answer. No date. No explanation of what it was for. Just: not yet, and here is
something to wear.

If you have prayed that question and got nothing back, the book has already put your exact
words into the mouths of the people closest to God in the whole vision, and left the silence
in. It did not tidy it up. Whatever else is true, you are not outside this story for asking.

The sixth seal shakes everything loose, and everyone - kings, generals, the rich, slaves -
hides and asks the same thing: *who can stand?*

# The answer to that question

Chapter seven answers it, and the answer is a crowd.

John hears a number counted off like a war census, tribe by tribe: 144,000. Then he turns to
look at this army and sees a crowd nobody can count, out of every nation and language, in
white, holding palm branches.

Palm branches are not weapons. They are what you wave when the fighting is over and you were
on the winning side. They come onto the field carrying victory signs because somebody else
won it.

They are described as people who came out of the great tribulation - not people who were
spared it. Read that plainly: the promise is not that it will not touch you.

# Warnings nobody heeds

Seven trumpets sound, and before the first one there is silence in heaven for about half an
hour - the singing stops, because the prayers of God's people are being brought in. The room
goes quiet to listen. Do with that what you like; it is what the text says.

Then disaster in thirds. A third, not everything - because these are warnings, and a warning
is aimed at someone who can still change.

And at the end of it, the sentence this section is built around: the rest of humanity did not
repent.

They saw it. They did not change. Not because they could not.

If you have watched people look straight at what they are doing and continue, the book is not
going to tell you that you misread them.

# Sweet, then bitter

An angel the size of a landscape comes down, one foot on sea and one on land, and seven
thunders speak - and John is told not to write it down. The one place in the book where he
is refused. Some of it you are not going to get.

He is handed a small scroll and told to eat it. Honey in the mouth, acid in the stomach.
Both. If the message has only ever tasted sweet to you, you have not swallowed it; if it only
ever tastes bitter, you have not tasted it. Then he is told to go and speak again.

# The part that will sound familiar

Two witnesses appear - a picture of the church. They do miracles, and nobody can touch them.

Then they are killed. Their bodies lie in the street. And the world celebrates. People send
each other gifts about it.

That is in the Bible. God's own witnesses lose publicly, and the public is delighted.

Three and a half days later they stand up, and this is the moment when large numbers of people
finally turn to God - not during the miracles. After the humiliation.

Here is why, and it is the reason this whole version exists. Most people are managing a
private fear that if they are ever truly helpless, that is the end - everything gone. Then
they watch someone be exactly that, publicly, with nothing left, and find that God was down
there too. And they think: then maybe it is not over for me either.

The miracles impressed people. The helplessness convinced them.

Then the seventh trumpet, and a shout: *the kingdom of the world has become the kingdom of
our Lord.* Not will become. Has. Said over two corpses in a street.

# The dragon who always loses

Now the story restarts from a great height. A woman about to give birth, and a red dragon
waiting to eat the child.

The dragon fails. The child is caught up to the throne, in half a sentence.

You know this story already - it is Christmas, told from above. Satan tried repeatedly to kill
Jesus, finally managed it, and Jesus got up.

Then war in heaven, and the dragon is thrown down. He loses that too. And he is furious
specifically because he knows his time is short. He goes after the woman, and the ground
itself opens and swallows the flood he sends.

The pattern is the point, and it is worth saying out loud if you are tired: however large he
looks, he is the one who loses. To an infant. To Michael. To dirt.

And the line that explains the rest of the book: *they overcame him by the blood of the Lamb
and by the word of their testimony.* Not by force. By what was already done, and by telling
the truth about it.

# The beast, and why it is convincing

Two beasts come up to help him. The first has a head wounded to death - and healed. The world
is amazed and follows it.

Note carefully why that is not the resurrection. The beast was never dead; it was wounded.
And people follow it out of fear, not love.

The second beast looks like a lamb and speaks like a dragon. It does miracles, and it makes
everyone wear a mark showing whose side they are on. Refuse, and you cannot buy or sell.

Three of them: dragon, beast, false prophet - a forgery of Father, Son, and Spirit. An
impressive forgery. That is what makes it work.

And the test the book gives is one you can actually use: *looks like a lamb, sounds like a
dragon.* Does it look kind and sound cruel? Does it promise you safety on condition that
somebody else is frightened? That is how you spot it - not by the branding, by the sound.

The number is 666. Seven is completeness in this book. Six is what falls short. Three sixes is
falling short three times. The number is a joke at their expense.

# Two harvests

Chapter fourteen opens with relief: the Lamb on Mount Zion, and that uncountable crowd with
him, singing.

Their weapon, stated plainly, is that they stayed. Not cleverness, not strength. They kept
trusting and never pretended not to know him. That is the whole armoury, and it is available
to anyone, which is the point.

Three angels fly past. The first has good news for every nation - the offer is still open.
The second says Babylon has fallen. The third gives a hard warning. Notice the order: the good
news goes first.

Then the harvest, in two parts. One is a gathering-in. The other is a winepress, for those who
wanted nothing to do with any of it. The book does not enjoy the second one, and neither will
you.

Why two? Because a choice with no consequences is not a choice. Fourteen chapters of invitation
come first. Nobody arrives at the winepress by accident.

# Wrath, correctly defined

Seven bowls, and here the word has to be fixed, because it has been used against you.

God's wrath is not him losing his temper. It is his refusal to stop before the thing is
actually fixed. It is a doctor who will not go home while the patient is unstable.

And look at what it targets - the list is specific, not indiscriminate: the mark of the beast;
the fact that God's witnesses suffered; hardness, twice, which is the refusal to admit you
were wrong; and finally the gathering at Armageddon.

The hardness is the part that goes quiet in the room. It becomes unbearably hot, then
unbearably dark, and people bite their tongues in pain - and still do not change their minds.
Three times the text says it. Not because they could not.

They gather at a place called Armageddon convinced they are fighting evil. Satan told them so.
Being certain you are on the right side is not the same as being on it.

The seventh bowl is poured, and a voice from the throne says one word: **It is done.** The same
word Jesus said on the cross.

# Babylon, and how she actually falls

Now the great city, drawn as a woman in purple and gold with a golden cup full of filth. She
tells the kings of the earth what to do and she profits from human suffering. She is not a
person. She is every arrangement that looks magnificent and runs on other people's pain.

Here is how she falls, and it is the strangest turn in the book.

God does not send an army. The beast and the kings she was riding turn on her and destroy her
themselves.

Evil eats itself. God's plan for her destruction consists largely of letting her be what she
is.

If you have been waiting for someone to come and break the thing that is grinding people
down, the book's answer is not the one you were expecting. It says the machine is
self-consuming and its own operators will finish it.

Then the kings and merchants stand at a distance and weep - and listen to what they say. It is
all about their lost trade. Nobody mourns *her*. They mourn their margins. That is all she
was ever worth to them.

And then, at last, a wedding. The bride is ready, dressed in fine linen, which the text stops
to explain: the linen is the good that God's people actually did. Not sentiment. Deeds.

Heaven opens and a rider comes out on a white horse. His robe is dipped in blood *before the
battle starts* - it is his own. He wins alone, with a sword that comes out of his mouth. He
speaks, and it is over.

# A thousand years, and one verse

Christians disagree about this chapter more than any other, and anyone who tells you it is
obvious is selling something.

Satan is chained so that he cannot do the thing he most wants, which is to deceive the
nations. And the church reigns with Christ - though our way of reigning looks, from outside,
almost exactly like losing. The text names people beheaded for their testimony and says these
are the ones who reign.

At the end he is released, gathers everything he has left, and attacks. And it evaporates.
Fire falls and it is finished, faster than any battle in the book. Twenty chapters of
preparation, and it takes one verse.

Then a great white throne, and books, and everyone standing there with no possibility of being
important. And one thing decides it.

You will want to ask about people you love. The honest answer is that we are not told
everything about who is in that book. We are told exactly what the one holding it is like: he
is the one who wept, who touched people nobody else would touch, and who died for the ones who
killed him.

# What the ending actually is

The new Jerusalem comes down, and it is three things at once - a city, a garden, and a bride.

Its length, width, and height are equal. A perfect cube. There was one other cube in the whole
Bible: the Holy of Holies, the one room where God was closest and almost nobody was allowed
in. The problem with the old temple was that you could be near or far depending which room you
stood in. So the new city is *entirely* Holy of Holies. There is no far part any more.

There is a river and a tree whose leaves are for the healing of the nations. That is Eden -
lost on the third page of the Bible, handed back on the last.

But notice what God does not do. He does not put everybody back in the garden and write off
the rest. After Eden, human beings built cities, out of fear, because they were on their own.
And God does not demolish the city. He moves into the middle of it.

He takes what people built out of fear and lives there.

That is the answer to the thing you have been carrying. Not that the world was fine. Not that
it gets replaced with something unrelated. That the world people made under the worst
conditions gets kept, and healed, and inhabited.

The sentence the whole book has been walking toward is this one: *Look - God's dwelling place
is now among the people, and he will dwell with them.*

Every tear wiped away. Not forgotten. Wiped - which means someone is close enough to do it,
and knows what each one was for.

# And then an invitation

The book does not end with a warning. It ends with an open door. The Spirit and the bride say,
*Come.* Whoever is thirsty, come. Whoever wishes, take the water of life, free.

Twenty-two chapters of dragons and beasts and trumpets and bowls, and the last word is *come*.

You are not asked to feel hopeful. You are asked whether you want a drink.
