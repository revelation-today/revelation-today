Here is the story of Revelation, start to finish, told the way the whole Church has always half-known it: as a liturgy. No footnotes, no jargon - just the story, and the worship at its center, which you may recognize.

## How everything started

In the beginning, God knelt in the dust, shaped a man with his own hands, and breathed his own breath into him. Adam woke into a garden humming with life, set there to tend it as a priest tends a sanctuary - the first temple, God walking among his own creation, receiving the praise first given to it.

One thing alone was not good: Adam was alone. So God built Eve out of Adam's own side, and for one shining moment, all of creation stood as it was meant to stand - a single act of worship, offered back to the God who made it.

It did not last. Adam desired the gift more than the Giver. That one small turning cracked the world in two, and the sanctuary was left standing empty.

Driven from the garden, Adam's children built sanctuaries of their own devising - cities, towers, kingdoms - offered to gods that could not hear them. But God had not abandoned his purpose of communion with them. He turned to Abraham, and called him to entrust himself entirely to a promise - even, in the end, the son he had waited a lifetime to receive.

Out of Abraham, God raised up a nation, Israel, and gave it what Adam had lost: a tabernacle, then a temple, incense rising morning and evening, a curtain guarding the Holy of Holies. Worship, restored - but only in shadow, only from a distance.

## The promise

Israel carried the promise, and the pattern too: would this people love the gifts more than the Giver, as Adam had? They did, until the covenant lay in ruins, the temple was destroyed, and the people were driven out, exiled just as Adam had once been.

But God was not finished. He promised a Redeemer - one who would build a kingdom, and a worship, that would never fall, a door standing open to anyone willing to walk through it.

## The vision (Ch. 1) {#vision}

Picture the scene: an old man, alone on a rocky island in the middle of the sea, exiled for refusing to bow before anyone but Christ. It is the Lord's Day - the same day kept holy in every parish and every parish church since - and John, even in banishment, keeps it as best he can. Then a voice behind him breaks like a trumpet, and everything changes.

He turns, and the vision overtakes him entirely: seven lampstands burning gold in the air, recalling the very lampstand that once stood in Israel's sanctuary, and standing among them, one impossible to describe and impossible to look away from. Eyes like fire. Feet like bronze glowing from the furnace. A voice like the roar of many waters, a face too radiant to behold directly, as though one were to stare into the sun. Seven stars rest in his right hand; a sword, sharp on both edges, issues from his mouth.

John falls before him like a dead man - the same posture the whole of Scripture asks of anyone who truly meets this glory, the posture still taken at the threshold of every sanctuary where the Lord is truly present.

Then a hand rests on his shoulder, and words undo the fear before it can finish forming: "Do not be afraid. I am the First and the Last, the Living One. I died - and behold, I am alive forever. And I hold the keys of Death and of Hades."

The truth breaks like sunrise: the kingdom long awaited has already arrived, and every believer - king and priest together - has been given a share in Christ's own priesthood, the very priesthood exercised at every altar where his sacrifice is proclaimed.

Before anything else may be said, one thing must be: this vision reveals what the Church exists to be - a lampstand kept alight not by her own strength, but sustained by the One who stands among the lamps. Nothing more is required of her; nothing less will do.

So: is she found ready, her lamp still burning, when he comes to walk among the lampstands again?

## The letters to the churches (Ch. 2-3) {#letters}

Seven churches, seven letters, seven mirrors held before seven very different congregations - each measured, still, by whether her lamp burns true before the One who walks among them.

Ephesus labors to exhaustion, sound in every doctrine, faithful in every duty - and has quietly let love slip through her fingers. Without it, all the labor comes to nothing, however correctly the liturgy is kept. Better, says the Lord, that this lampstand be removed than to persist so.

Smyrna could not be more different: poor, despised, pressed on every side by a hostile city - and one of only two churches to receive nothing but commendation.

Pergamum holds her ground in the very shadow of Satan's throne. Some die for it. Yet within her walls, false teaching has crept in unopposed, quietly leading the flock astray from sound worship.

In Thyatira, the corruption begins at the top: the shepherds themselves have wandered from the fold they were set to guard.

Sardis appears alive from without - her forms perhaps still observed. Within, she is already dead, persuaded she can manage without God, and failing at nearly everything as a result.

Philadelphia is the other church commended without reservation: small, depleted, with nothing left in her treasury, and faithful still - and to her the Lord promises a place as a pillar in the temple of his God, never again to go out from it.

Then there is Laodicea: lukewarm, self-satisfied, blind to her own poverty - the only church to receive no praise at all. And yet even at her door, the Lord does not depart. He knocks. He warns. He still desires to enter and to dine with her, to keep supper with her as with a friend - the first small foretaste, here, of the wedding feast the whole book is moving toward. To the one who conquers, he promises a seat beside him on his own throne, the way the redeemed will one day stand nearest the altar of the Lamb.

Seven lampstands, seven calls to watch and to worship rightly - each letter closing with the same refrain: let anyone with an ear hear what the Spirit says to the churches.

## Worship (Ch. 4-5) {#worship}

It falls to every believer to rise and prepare for what is coming. But how does one prepare for something of this magnitude?

The Lord's answer is not a strategy. It is a liturgy. A door stands open in heaven, and John is caught up through it into the room where every creature and every crown finds its true position before God - the very room from which every act of true worship on earth has always taken its pattern, the sanctuary that every altar on earth is only a copy and a shadow of.

Around the throne, four living creatures cry without ceasing, day and night: "Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God Almighty, who was, and is, and is to come." This is the same acclamation still sung at the heart of the Mass and the Divine Liturgy, the Sanctus lifted at every altar where bread and wine are offered - the very words, the very cry, carried unbroken from that throne room into this one. Twenty-four elders fall down before the One seated there and cast their crowns before the throne, for worship costs something: it means laying down every crown of one's own, holding nothing back.

Then a scroll appears, sealed with seven seals, in the right hand of the One on the throne, and no one anywhere is found worthy to open it - until an elder says: weep no longer, for the Lion of the tribe of Judah has conquered. John looks for a lion. He sees a Lamb, standing as though slain, and at the sight every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth falls down to worship him: "Worthy is the Lamb who was slain, to receive power and wealth and wisdom and might and honor and glory and blessing." Golden bowls of incense are lifted in the hands of the elders - "which are the prayers of the saints," John is told plainly - the same incense that still rises from the censer at every Vespers, every solemn Mass, carrying the prayers of the faithful upward exactly as it did before that throne.

He is the hero of the whole story: worthy, when no one else was found worthy, to open what needed opening and to rule what needed ruling. Once a soul has passed through that open door and knelt before that Lamb, there is no return to who one was before.

## The seals (Ch. 6) {#seals}

The seals break, one by one, each release loosing something new upon the earth - and even here, in the unsealing of catastrophe, the scene remains the throne room: nothing is loosed except what the Lamb himself unseals.

First, a white horse gallops forth - a rider armed with a bow, crowned, already bent on conquest. For a moment it resembles victory. It is counterfeit: Christ is not the only claimant to human worship, and this rider borrows the color of the true King's horse to sell a lie. Strip the costume away, and violence alone remains. Behind the white horse comes a blood-red one, and peace is stripped from the earth like a scab, neighbor turning on neighbor. Behind the red horse, a black one, its rider holding a pair of scales - a day's wages for a day's bread, while oil and wine are kept safe for those who can still afford them. Behind the black horse, one the color of ash, and its rider's name is Death, with the grave riding close behind.

Conquest gives way to war, war to famine, famine to death.

Then the fifth seal breaks, and what it uncovers is not an army but an altar - the same altar beneath which incense and sacrifice have burned throughout the whole vision - and beneath it, the souls of those slain for their testimony, crying with one voice: how long, O Sovereign Lord, holy and true, until you judge the earth and avenge our blood? Each is given a white robe, the same robe worn by every soul who stands redeemed before the throne, and told to rest a little longer. Even here, beneath the altar, the posture remains worship, not despair - a martyrdom offered up rather than merely endured.

The sky itself answers next: the sun turns black as sackcloth, the moon becomes as blood, the stars fall like unripe figs shaken loose in a gale, and the heavens are rolled back like a scroll. The world already drowns in what it has brought upon itself, and the Church stands amid the wreckage, crying out still - not abandoning the liturgy even here, but carrying it, like incense, straight into the ruin.

## The army of God (Ch. 7) {#army}

Before matters worsen, something else occurs: God's own are sealed - marked upon the forehead, claimed in the midst of the storm as unmistakably his, exactly as every believer is marked at the font and at confirmation, set apart before the altar as belonging to the Lord.

Then John sees the multitude that seal has gathered: a great throng no one can number, from every nation and tribe and people and tongue, standing before the throne and before the Lamb, robed in white, palm branches in their hands - the same white robes worn by every soul beneath the altar, the same procession echoed whenever a congregation processes with palms, whenever the newly baptized are clothed in white at the font. They cry out with a great voice: "Salvation belongs to our God, who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb." The angels and elders and living creatures fall on their faces before the throne and worship, saying: "Amen! Blessing and glory and wisdom and thanksgiving and honor and power and might be to our God forever and ever. Amen." This is the same sevenfold doxology still echoed in every solemn "Glory be" offered at the end of a psalm.

One elder asks who these are, robed in white, and answers his own question: these are the ones who have come out of the great tribulation, who have washed their robes and made them white in the blood of the Lamb. Therefore they stand before the throne of God and serve him day and night in his temple - the eternal liturgy, unbroken, of which every earthly Divine Liturgy is already a participation. He who sits on the throne will shelter them; they will hunger no more, thirst no more, and God himself will wipe away every tear from their eyes.

This is the army of God - though it fights like no army ever assembled. No swords, no borrowed violence, only ceaseless worship and a loyalty nothing can exhaust. Whatever follows, this much is already settled: whose side has been chosen, and whose liturgy is being kept.

## The trumpets (Ch. 8-9) {#trumpets}

Silence falls in heaven for about half an hour - the same hush that still settles over a sanctuary at the elevation, the same stillness kept before the Blessed Sacrament - and into that silence, an angel steps forward with a golden censer, and much incense is given him, to offer with the prayers of all the saints upon the golden altar before the throne. The smoke of the incense rises before God, mingled with those prayers, exactly as it still rises today from every censer swung before an altar at Vespers, at the Divine Liturgy, at Benediction. Then the same angel takes that same censer, fills it with fire from the altar, and hurls it down upon the earth, and thunder and lightning and an earthquake answer.

Now it is the Church's turn to shake the world - not with weapons, but the way the Lord himself always worked: through what looks like weakness. The trumpets sound, one after another, and the earth trembles in answer to the Church's own prayer, carried up as incense and returned as judgment.

Food fails. Water turns bitter. Trade collapses. Security vanishes. Surely now humanity will look up and ask what is real, and turn at last toward the altar it has forsaken.

It does not. Even as their idols turn against them - even as those same idols strip away every last shred of hope, until death itself looks like relief - people cling tighter rather than let go, still bowing before gods of gold and silver and stone that can neither see nor hear nor walk. Suffering alone has never yet been sufficient to change a human heart or turn it back toward true worship. Repentance is not manufactured by hardship; it is a gift received, or refused, before the throne.

## The small scroll (Ch. 10) {#scroll}

The first strike has failed. Hardship can crack open almost anything - except a heart set on the wrong altar.

Now a mighty angel descends, wrapped in cloud, a rainbow over his head, his face like the sun and his legs like pillars of fire - the same rainbow that once circled the throne itself, now brought down to earth. He sets one foot on the sea and one on the land, and cries out with a voice like a lion roaring, and seven thunders answer. John is about to write down what they said, but is told instead to seal it up - a reminder that not every mystery of the liturgy is given to be explained; some are only given to be received.

Then the angel holds out a little scroll, opened, and tells John to take it and eat it. It will be sweet as honey in his mouth, but bitter in his stomach once swallowed. John does exactly this - takes the word of God bodily into himself, the way a communicant takes the Host, sweetness first upon the tongue, and only afterward the weight of what has truly been received settling into the whole of a life.

This is not another disaster added to the ones already loosed. It is a revelation in the fullest sense: something true about God himself, offered to be tasted and digested rather than merely observed, about to accomplish through testimony what judgment alone could never accomplish. John is told he must prophesy again, about many peoples and nations and languages and kings - the scroll consumed is not the end of his commission but its renewal, sending him back out to bear witness.

The lesson for every reader is the same one every altar teaches: the word of God is not meant only to be heard, but eaten - taken in until it becomes part of the one who receives it, sweet in the promise, costly in the living out. What looked, moments before, like unrelenting judgment now turns toward testimony, toward two witnesses about to stand and speak in the very next scene.

## The two witnesses (Ch. 11) {#witnesses}

John is given a measuring rod and told to measure the temple of God and the altar, and those who worship there - but not the outer court, which is given over to the nations to trample. Step past the veil, and both worlds appear as they truly are. The temple is not a place one earns entry into by merit; it is the place where loyalty either shows itself in worship, or does not.

Two witnesses are given power to prophesy, clothed in sackcloth, standing as the two olive trees and two lampstands before the Lord of the earth - lampstands again, the same image that opened the whole vision, now embodied in flesh and blood. They testify with everything they possess, fire proceeding from their mouths to consume their enemies, until at last the beast that rises from the abyss makes war on them, conquers them, and kills them. Their bodies lie unburied in the street of the great city, and the peoples of the earth refuse them burial, and instead hold festival over the corpses, exchanging gifts, rejoicing that these two tormentors are silenced at last.

For three and a half days, it looks like total defeat - the same interval, broken and incomplete, that runs through so much of this book, always falling short of the fullness God alone holds.

Then the breath of life from God enters them, and they stand on their feet, and great fear falls on those who see them. A voice from heaven says, "Come up here," and they ascend to heaven in a cloud, in full sight of their enemies - a resurrection witnessed rather than merely reported.

This is exactly the pattern the Church herself is called to live: not a victory that bypasses suffering, but one that walks straight through it and stands upright on the far side, in full view of a watching world. What looks, for three and a half days, like the world's final word over faithful witness is never permitted to be the last word. The tomb is never the end of the liturgy; it is only ever the middle of it.

## The victory of Jesus (Ch. 12) {#jesus}

Now the same story is told again, drawn back to reveal the frame surrounding it - a very different nativity, enacted across the sky itself. A woman appears, clothed with the sun, the moon beneath her feet, a crown of twelve stars circling her head. She is with child, crying out in the pangs of labor - the Church, and Israel before her, bearing forth the Christ into a world already lying in wait to devour him.

Then the sky tears open again, and something far worse appears: an enormous red dragon, seven crowned heads, ten horns, a tail vast enough to sweep a third of the stars from heaven and cast them to the earth. He stations himself before the woman, waiting - the instant the child is born, he intends to devour him whole.

The child is born regardless - a son who will rule every nation with an iron scepter - and before the dragon's jaws can close, the child is caught up to God's own throne, safe beyond the reach of every enemy. War breaks out in heaven: Michael and his angels against the dragon and his, and the dragon is defeated. No place remains for him there any longer. He is cast down to the earth, and a voice rings out, declaring that salvation and power and the kingdom of God have come, and the authority of his Christ, for the accuser of the brothers has been thrown down - the one who accused them day and night before our God. He is overcome by the blood of the Lamb and by the word of the testimony of those who did not cling to their own lives in the face of him.

Enraged, and knowing his time is short, the dragon turns on the woman - and when she is carried to a place prepared for her, he turns his fury upon the rest of her offspring, all who keep the commandments of God and hold fast the testimony of Jesus.

Take courage nonetheless: overcoming the dragon was never the Church's task. Christ has already accomplished it in heaven, before the battle ever reached the earth. The task now given is simply to keep bearing witness, to keep drawing others toward the altar where that victory is proclaimed - even while the dragon, knowing exactly how little time remains to him, rages harder than ever before.

## The dragon and the two beasts (Ch. 13) {#beasts}

Consider the dragon's record so far:
- He sought to kill Christ, and failed.
- He sought to keep his place in heaven, and was cast out.
- He sought to destroy Israel, and failed again.

So he reaches for new instruments - two beasts, one clawing up out of the sea, one crawling up out of the earth. The first is given the dragon's own power and throne and great authority, and the whole earth marvels and worships the dragon and the beast, asking, "Who is like the beast, and who can fight against it?" - a mockery of the very acclamation that belongs, rightly, only before the throne of the Lamb. It blasphemes God's name and his dwelling, those who dwell in heaven, and makes war on the saints.

The second beast rises from the earth, deceiving by wonders and signs, causing the earth to worship the first beast, even making an image of it and demanding that all - small and great, rich and poor, free and slave - be marked, and that none may buy or sell without that mark. Here is the counterfeit sacrament, the mark set against the seal already placed on the foreheads of the redeemed: a false christening offered by a false priesthood, for whoever the beast is, he is a grotesque copy of God, Christ, and the Spirit, close enough to fool almost everyone, demanding the very worship that belongs to the Lamb alone.

But look closer, and the imitation is already cracking. The beast conquers only by violence, dazzles only by trickery, and builds nothing that will last, because the One it is copying has already won, permanently, at the cross. Every altar it raises is hollow; every liturgy it stages is theater without substance, sound without a throne behind it. Here is a call for the endurance and faith of the saints: to know a false sanctuary when one meets it, and to keep worship for the One to whom it is due.

## The two harvests (Ch. 14) {#harvest}

John looks, and there stands the Lamb on Mount Zion, and with him the hundred and forty-four thousand who bear his name and his Father's name written on their foreheads - the true seal, set against the counterfeit mark just given by the beast. From the throne comes a sound like many waters, like loud thunder, and like harpists playing their harps, and they sing a new song before the throne, before the four living creatures and the elders - a song no one else can learn, for it belongs only to those redeemed from the earth. These are the ones who follow the Lamb wherever he goes, kept undefiled, the firstfruits offered to God and to the Lamb.

Christ has already won this at the cross, and the Church has kept faith with that victory ever since - and now the enemy's forces begin to scatter like smoke. An angel flies overhead with an eternal gospel to proclaim to every nation and tribe and tongue and people, crying with a loud voice: "Fear God and give him glory... and worship him who made heaven and earth, the sea and the springs of water." Worship remains, even here, the dividing line running through the whole of the story. Another voice follows, announcing that Babylon the great has fallen, and a third warns of the fate awaiting any who worship the beast and his image and receive his mark - to be tormented with fire and sulfur in the presence of the holy angels and the Lamb. Here is the call for the endurance of the saints, those who keep the commandments of God and their faith in Jesus.

Not everyone gathered into that harvest is bound for the same end. Two harvests unfold: one Son of Man, seated on a white cloud, swings a sickle and reaps the earth's ripe grain. Then another angel swings a sickle of his own, gathering the earth's grapes into the great winepress of the wrath of God.

Two sickles sweep across the earth - one harvest gathered in for the wedding feast, one given over to judgment. Truth ceases to be theoretical.

## The bowls of wrath (Ch. 15-16) {#bowls}

Before the bowls are ever poured, John sees another sight in heaven: those who conquered the beast and its image and the number of its name, standing beside a sea of glass mingled with fire, holding harps of God, and singing the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb - two songs become one, the whole of salvation history folded into a single hymn: "Great and amazing are your deeds, O Lord God the Almighty! Just and true are your ways, O King of the nations! Who will not fear you, O Lord, and glorify your name? For you alone are holy." Every nation will come and worship before God, for his righteous acts have been revealed.

Then the temple of the tent of witness in heaven is opened, and out of it come seven angels bearing seven golden bowls full of the wrath of God, and the temple is filled with smoke from the glory of God and from his power, so that no one can enter it until the seven plagues are finished - the sanctuary itself given over, for a time, to judgment rather than intercession.

Now God's righteous zeal pours out as something long withheld and finally released, bowl after bowl, wave after wave, until the kingdom built on falsehood has nowhere left to stand. Every bowl lands exactly where it is aimed: on false worship, on cruelty shown toward God's people, on hearts too hardened to break even now. The angel of the waters declares it just: those who shed the blood of saints and prophets have been given blood to drink, for they deserve it. And the altar itself answers: "Yes, Lord God the Almighty, true and just are your judgments" - the altar, which has carried the martyrs' prayers since the fifth seal, now vindicating them at last.

The armies of the earth gather for one last stand, at a place whose name has become a byword for disaster: Armageddon. They come certain of victory, gathered by demonic spirits performing signs, in one final grotesque liturgy raised against the true one.

They are already finished. They simply do not yet know it.

## The harlot (Ch. 17-19) {#harlot}

One deception still waits to be unveiled before the story can close - and unveiling it discloses what this has always been about: not merely the defeat of evil, but God binding himself to his people forever, as in a marriage.

Here is the deception: a woman, resplendent, seated on a scarlet beast, draped in purple and scarlet, adorned in gold and jewels and pearls that rightly belonged to the bride, holding a golden cup full of abominations, offering intoxication to any who will drink from it. She is named on her forehead: Babylon the great, mother of harlots and of earth's abominations. Draw back the finery, though, and the beast stands beneath it, teeth bared. She is no bride. She is a harlot, drunk on the blood of the saints and the martyrs of Jesus - and many even within the visible Church have been seduced by the performance, mistaking her splendor for glory.

Her final act appears flawless. It is not. The very powers the adversary sends to destroy the Church turn instead and tear the harlot apart, make her desolate and naked, devour her flesh, and burn her up with fire - his own weapon, turned against him. Heaven rejoices over her fall: "Fallen, fallen is Babylon the great," and a voice cries, "Come out of her, my people, lest you take part in her sins."

The way is finally clear to finish him for good.

At this, heaven itself breaks into liturgy: a great multitude cries "Alleluia! Salvation and glory and power belong to our God" - the very word still sung at every Easter Vigil and every Paschal liturgy, unbroken from that hour to this - and the twenty-four elders and four living creatures fall down and worship God, saying, "Amen. Alleluia!" A voice from the throne calls all his servants to praise him, and a great multitude answers like the roar of many waters and mighty peals of thunder: "Alleluia! For the Lord our God the Almighty reigns. Let us rejoice and be glad and give him the glory, for the marriage of the Lamb has come, and his Bride has made herself ready." Blessed are those invited to the wedding supper of the Lamb - the same invitation extended, still, at every altar rail, in the words that echo it even now: "Blessed are those called to the supper of the Lamb."

## The millenium (Ch. 20) {#1000y}

It is accomplished, even where it went unseen. All along, while the Church appeared to be losing, she was reigning - not with the kind of power anyone was watching for, but with the quiet authority of those who worship rightly while the world rages. John sees thrones, and those seated on them are given authority to judge; and the souls of those beheaded for the testimony of Jesus and the word of God come to life and reign with Christ. This is the first resurrection, and blessed and holy is anyone who shares in it: over such the second death has no power, but they will be priests of God and of Christ, and will reign with him.

That reign, hidden as it was, has shaped the outcome of everything. It is the reign of those who kept the liturgy of faithfulness even under the beast's own altar-mockery, refusing his mark, refusing his counterfeit worship, and so refusing, quietly, all along, to bow anywhere but before the one true throne.

When the appointed time is finished, the deceiver is loosed once more for a little while, gathers the nations for battle, surrounds the camp of the saints and the beloved city - and fire comes down from heaven and consumes them. The deceiver is thrown into the lake of fire and sulfur, joining the beast and the false prophet, to be tormented day and night forever and ever. His long war against the woman and her offspring, against every altar and every lamp raised in testimony, ends exactly as it always would: cast out for good, exactly as he once was cast out of heaven.

Now comes the last reckoning: a great white throne, from whose presence earth and sky flee away, and no place is found for them. The dead, great and small, stand before that throne, and books are opened, and another book too, the book of life, and the dead are judged by what is written in the books, by what they have done. Whoever is not found written in the book of life is thrown into the lake of fire - the second death.

It is decided, once for all, who spends eternity before that throne in endless worship, and who has chosen, all along, not to.

## The new heaven and new earth (Ch. 21-22) {#paradise}

And then: a wedding. A city comes down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband, and a loud voice from the throne declares what the whole story has waited to hear: "Behold, the dwelling place of God is with man. He will dwell with them, and they will be his people, and God himself will be with them as their God. He will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain anymore, for the former things have passed away."

The city itself nearly exceeds description: a wall of jasper, streets of pure gold, clear as glass, foundations adorned with every kind of jewel, twelve gates, each a single pearl, never shut, for there is no night there. No temple stands anywhere within it, for the Lord God the Almighty and the Lamb are its temple - the last shadow finally given way to the substance it always pointed toward, every earthly sanctuary fulfilled and no longer needed. Neither sun nor moon is required, for the glory of God gives it light, and its lamp is the Lamb - the lampstands of the very first vision now gathered up into this single, undying flame.

Through the middle of the street flows a river of the water of life, clear as crystal, flowing from the throne of God and of the Lamb, and on either side of the river stands the tree of life, bearing twelve kinds of fruit, yielding fruit each month, its leaves for the healing of the nations. No longer will there be anything accursed, for the throne of God and of the Lamb will be in the city, and his servants will worship him. They will see his face, and his name will be on their foreheads. And there will be no more night; they need no light of lamp or sun, for the Lord God will be their light, and they will reign forever and ever.

There the eternal liturgy is fulfilled at last - no longer a shadow glimpsed through incense and candlelight, but the throne room itself, entered fully, forever: the Sanctus sung not by a congregation gathered for an hour, but by a Bride united with her Bridegroom for good, no more distance left to close.

And now, having heard the story - go, and keep the feast. "Surely I am coming soon." Amen. Come, Lord Jesus.
