# The Four Horsemen and the Wrath of the Lamb

In the last session we stood in the throne room, where everything answers to God. In chapter 5 a scroll sealed with seven seals is handed to the Lamb, the only one found worthy to open it. Now he opens it, and the first thing the seals reveal is not the end of the world but the power the churches were actually up against. Smyrna's faithfulness unto death and Philadelphia's patient endurance did not happen in a vacuum. They happened under riders who promised victory and delivered something else. Revelation 6 unmasks those riders, and only then does it let us hear the cry of the people who paid for them.

## A rider who looks like a victor

As the Lamb opens the first four seals, four horsemen ride out one at a time. A rider on a white horse who goes out "as a conqueror bent on conquest" (Rev 6:2); a rider on a red horse who takes peace from the earth (Rev 6:4); a rider on a black horse who inflicts crushing food prices on the poor while luxury goods are protected (Rev 6:5-6); and a rider on a pale horse named Death, with Hades following (Rev 6:8).

The first rider is deliberately unsettling, because on the surface he can look like Christ — riding out with a bow, given a crown, described only as "conquering." White is the colour Christ himself rides in at the end of the book (Rev 19:11). But look at what follows him: war, then engineered scarcity that crushes the poor while protecting the comfortable, then death. This is not the pattern of Jesus's kingdom. It is a counterfeit — evil dressed up to look like triumph, making grand promises before its true face is unmasked in the devastation that follows. You do not recognise this rider by his horse. You recognise him by what rides out behind him.

## Who pays

Many of you know this pattern from your own history, not from a book. Powers have come into this region more than once promising liberation, order, security or prosperity. Some arrived under religious banners, some under secular ones. What followed was often the same sequence: whoever did not cheer became the enemy; the cost of the new order fell on those with the least, while the comfortable found their oil and wine untouched; and then came the quiet dying of villages, of communities, of churches that had stood for centuries.

Notice especially the third rider. His scales do not create a natural famine; they create an unjust one. Bread is priced out of reach, and the luxuries are kept safe. Economic pressure on those who will not conform is one of the oldest weapons there is, and Revelation names it as one of the riders. When Christians are squeezed out of jobs, markets and neighbourhoods for refusing to go along, that is not a side issue. It is the black horse.

And notice who sends the riders out. None of them comes on his own. Each appears only when the Lamb opens a seal. Their havoc is not outside God's reach; it is being exposed as part of the Lamb's work, and it does not get the last word.

## The cry under the altar

Now the fifth seal shows the price of all this. John sees "under the altar the souls of those who had been slain because of the word of God and the testimony they had maintained. They called out in a loud voice, 'How long, Sovereign Lord, holy and true, until you judge the inhabitants of the earth and avenge our blood?'" (Rev 6:9-10).

These are the people who would not ride along. They refused the counterfeit conqueror, and they were killed for it — not symbolically, but killed. Their cry comes exactly here, after four seals of deception, violence, injustice and death, because it is the cry of the riders' victims. And their response, from heaven itself, in the presence of God, is not patient silence. It is a cry. A demand, almost: how long. If you have ever felt that this question was too raw, too impatient, too much like doubt to say out loud in prayer, hear this plainly: it is already in Scripture, spoken by the martyrs themselves, directly to God, and God does not rebuke them for asking it.

## God's answer is not silence

"Then each of them was given a white robe, and they were told to wait a little longer, until the full number of their fellow servants, their brothers and sisters, were killed just as they had been" (Rev 6:11). This is not the answer we might wish for — an immediate end to suffering. But look carefully at what it is not: it is not silence, and it is not a rebuke. God answers. He gives them white robes — a mark of vindication and honor, given now, not deferred until some unspecified future. And he tells them the truth: a little longer, until the full number is complete.

This is a hard word, and it should not be preached as an easy one. But it is also, in its own way, the most direct comfort this book offers a community that has waited generations for justice: your cry has been heard. It reached God. It was not lost, ignored, or dismissed. You are not shouting into an empty sky. Somewhere in heaven, other believers who died before you are already vindicated, already clothed in white, already told that their wait — like yours — is not endless, even if it is not yet over.

## The wrath that is not what people fear

Later in the same chapter, we're told that when the full weight of God's response finally arrives, "the kings of the earth, the princes, the generals, the rich, the mighty, and everyone else... called to the mountains and the rocks, 'Fall on us and hide us... from the wrath of the Lamb!'" (Rev 6:15-16). Notice something important here: this is a description of how the guilty perceive Jesus's justice, not necessarily a description of what that justice will actually look like when it arrives. Their terror comes from their own guilt, the same way Adam and Eve hid from God after the fall, and Israel hid from God in shame over its own unfaithfulness in Hosea. The text shows us their fear. It does not show us God taking pleasure in it.

The word translated "wrath" here — orge in Greek — describes something that has built up slowly over a long time, not a sudden, short-tempered outburst. This is not an impulsive God losing his temper. It is patient, long-building justice, finally acting on behalf of those who have suffered under real oppression for a very long time.

## Wrath as justice for real victims, not abstraction

It is worth being direct about something many comfortable readers of this book never have to reckon with honestly: God's wrath, in Revelation, exists specifically because real people suffer real injustice — economic exploitation, slavery, persecution for their faith or their ethnicity, war, famine engineered by the greed of the powerful. In other words: because of what the riders do. If you have not lived any of that, God's wrath can feel like an uncomfortable, even embarrassing topic to preach. But if you have lived it, or your family has, or your church has, this is not embarrassing. It is exactly what you have been waiting to hear someone say plainly: God sees this. God is not neutral about this. God will act.

This is not a promise that gives license for personal revenge, or for violence carried out in Jesus's name — nothing in the New Testament supports that, and Revelation's own vision of victory, as we will see in the sessions ahead, comes through faithful witness and endurance, not through the church taking up the sword. That would only be riding out on a red horse of our own. But it is a promise that the suffering you have endured has not escaped God's notice, and it will not go unanswered forever.

## Hold both truths together

So hold both things Revelation gives us in this chapter, without letting either one cancel the other. First: the riders are unmasked. Every power that promises victory and leaves war, hunger and death behind it is shown for what it is, however white its horse, and the faithful are called not to ride along with it. Second: those who pay for refusing are not forgotten. Their "how long" is a legitimate, faithful prayer, one that heaven itself is already praying alongside you, and God's answer is not silence, even when it is not yet the full ending we long for. He sees. He answers. He marks his suffering people with honor now, even in the waiting, and he has promised that the waiting has an end.

**For reflection and prayer together:**

- Where in your own community's story do you see the pattern of the four horsemen — deception dressed as triumph, followed by real suffering for the vulnerable?
- Which promises of victory, security or prosperity are pressing on you to go along today, and who is paying the price of wheat while the oil and wine stay untouched?
- The souls under the altar were killed for not riding along, and God heard their cry. What would it look like this week to stay faithful where going along would be easier, and to bring your own "how long" honestly to God?
