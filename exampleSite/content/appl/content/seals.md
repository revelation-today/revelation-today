Conquest gives way to war, war to famine, famine to death - and underneath the altar, the souls of the faithful cry out with one honest question: how long, O Lord, until you judge the earth? They aren't rebuked for asking. They're told to wait, just a little longer.

Almost everyone is carrying a "how long" of their own. The diagnosis that hasn't changed after the third round of treatment. The marriage that has been "nearly better" for four years. The child who doesn't call. The war that is still on the news, months after everyone agreed it was unbearable. The colleague who lies and keeps getting promoted for it.

There are two usual ways out of that, and this chapter takes neither. The first is to pretend it is fine - to reach for the tidy sentence about everything happening for a reason, which mostly serves to end the conversation. The second is to go quiet: not a dramatic loss of faith, just a slow drift until you stop expecting anything and stop asking. The souls under the altar do neither. They say the hard thing out loud, to God, in the room where he is - and then they stay.

Notice what they get back. Not an explanation. Nobody tells them what the suffering was for, or how the pieces fit. They are given a white robe - the verdict that they were right, in the face of a world that said otherwise - and the word "wait". If you have decided that you will trust God again once you have the explanation, this chapter quietly suggests you may be waiting for the wrong thing. What is on offer here is a verdict and a presence, and only afterwards, much later in the book, an end.

And the wait is real. "A little longer" has now run for a very long time, and the honest reading of that is not that your question is too small to answer, but that the same delay that keeps you waiting is the space in which other people still get to change their minds.

So this week: say the actual question, in the words you would use if nobody religious were listening, and say it to God rather than about him. Tell one person who can hold it without rushing to fix it. And when you catch yourself either tidying the question away or letting it go silent, treat that as the moment to bring it back, not as proof that faith isn't working.

## Questions to reflect on

- What's the "how long, O Lord" question you've been sitting on without actually bringing it to God directly?
- Waiting for an answer is different from giving up on one. Which of those two has your own response to unresolved suffering actually looked like lately?
- Would you still want God if the answer never came, and what you got instead was the robe and his company?

## Prayer

Lord, how long? You know the questions I have been carrying without bringing them to you. Here they are. Thank you that you do not rebuke me for asking. Help me to wait with you instead of walking away. Amen.
